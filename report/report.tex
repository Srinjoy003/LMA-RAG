\noindent\textbf{Scope of this document.} The live numerical results below are the historical
24-candidate B1/A2 experiment on the pre-review bank. The dated implementation update
in Section~\ref{sec:implementation-update} describes later code and offline checks; it does
not replace those historical results with a completed revised-bank experiment.

\begin{abstract}
We are building an interviewer for junior technical screening in Python, SQL and data structures.
It keeps an estimate of the candidate's level in each skill, picks each question to reduce its
uncertainty, stops when it is confident, and works out \emph{why} a wrong answer is wrong. It
recommends fit, not fit or human review and never makes the hiring decision. For this report we
ran the full system (A2) and a fixed 12-question interview (B1) on the same \nCandidates{}
simulated candidates, with Gemini grading and a different model playing the candidate. The
adaptive system placed \accOurs{} of skill levels correctly against \accBase{} for the fixed
interview, a gain of \accGap{} points (95\% interval \accGapLow{} to \accGapHigh{} points). But it
also asked more: \exchOurs{} exchanges against \exchBase{}, mostly follow-ups after wrong answers.
So the live gain mixes adaptive question choice with extra questions. In simulated runs with a
perfect grader, adaptive choice at exactly 12 questions beat the fixed list by about 6 points; how
much of the live gain it explains is not yet measured. It confirmed \found{} of the
\planted{} planted misconceptions, which the fixed interview cannot do, and rarely named one on a
correctly answered reply (\falseAlarmOurs{}). However, only \precisionOurs{} of the misconceptions it
confirmed were planted ones; most of the rest came from wrong answers, and nearly all were
confirmed from a single question. Our proposal promised 25\% \emph{fewer} exchanges; we do not meet
that, and we explain why we chose accuracy over length.
\end{abstract}

\section{What we set out to do}

A screening interview has to judge several skills with a handful of questions. Fixed question
lists waste questions on skills that are already clear and leave others under-tested. LLM
interviewers make screening cheap, but they decide what to ask and when to stop from a prompt
alone, with no record of how sure they are. Adaptive testing solves this for multiple-choice
tests by asking the most informative question and stopping once the estimate is precise
\cite{zhuang2024}, and TestAgent \cite{yu2025} brings the idea to conversational assessments,
reporting about 20\% fewer questions. We apply it to open answers across several skills, and add a model of the candidate's
misconceptions. Our proposal asked two questions:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline]
  \item[RQ1] Can uncertainty-driven questioning with early stopping match a fixed 12-question
  interview's accuracy with fewer exchanges?
  \item[RQ2] Does modelling what the candidate \emph{believes} (their misconceptions) find those
  misconceptions better than a scorecard that only tracks skill?
\end{description}

All four parts of the system are now built and tested: the question bank, the interview engine,
the grader and diagnoser, and the evaluation harness with simulated candidates. This report covers
the first live experiment. It compares B1 and A2. The ablations that answer RQ2 directly
(A4, skill-only scorecard; A5, job-blind retrieval) are built but not yet run live.

\needspace{5\baselineskip}
\paragraph{Main findings so far.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The adaptive system judges levels clearly better than the fixed interview
  (\accOurs{} against \accBase{}). Observed verdict accuracy was similar, but with \nCandidates{}
  candidates we can show neither that A2's verdicts are better nor that they are no worse.
  \item It is \lengthChange\% longer, so part of the accuracy gain comes from asking more. In
  controlled runs at equal length, adaptive question choice alone still beat the fixed list
  (Section~\ref{sec:equal-length}).
  \item It finds about half of the planted misconceptions (\recallOurs{}) and rarely names one on a
  correct answer, but its confirmed misconceptions are mostly not planted ones (precision
  \precisionOurs{}). Section~\ref{sec:discussion} explains where they come from.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:loop} shows the loop. The LLM is used only where language is needed. Everything
that controls the interview (which question, when to stop, the verdict rule) is plain code, so the
candidate cannot change the procedure by asking. The grades and diagnoses still come from an LLM,
so a persuasive answer could in principle sway them; our safety check (two manipulation attempts,
Section~\ref{sec:results}) is too small to rule that out.

\paragraph{Scorecard.} Each skill has an ability value $\theta$ on a logit scale. Questions come in
three levels with difficulty $b = -1, 0, +1$. An answer earns 0, 1 or 2 marks, and we model the
chance of each mark with the Partial Credit Model \cite{masters1982}:
\[
  P(X = x \mid \theta, b) \;\propto\; \exp\Big(\sum_{k \le x} (\theta - \delta_k)\Big),
  \qquad \delta_1 = b - 0.5,\; \delta_2 = b + 0.5 .
\]
We start from a normal prior (mean 0, standard deviation 1) and update it on a grid after every
graded question. The estimate is the posterior mean (EAP, \cite{bock1982}) and the uncertainty is
the posterior standard deviation (SE). Each answer is graded three times; if the three marks
disagree, the answer counts as weaker evidence (2 of 3 agreeing gives weight 0.67).

\paragraph{From ability to levels.} The continuous estimate is turned into a level 0--3 with three
cut points on the same scale (Section~\ref{sec:tuning} explains how they were set):

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
Ability $\theta$ & below $-1.25$ & $-1.25$ to $-0.25$ & $-0.25$ to $0.75$ & $0.75$ and above \\
Reported level & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The same cut points link a job's minimum level to the ability scale: a minimum of level
$m$ means $\theta$ must be at or above the lower edge of level $m$ ($-1.25$ for level 1, $-0.25$ for
level 2, $0.75$ for level 3; a minimum of 0 is always met).

\paragraph{Choosing the next question.} The next skill is the one with the highest SE. The
level is the one whose difficulty is closest to that skill's estimate. Among the unasked bank
questions of that skill and level, the system prefers, in order: a question listing a misconception
it already suspects; a question on a topic the candidate just got wrong; a question covering a
topic the job needs that has not been tested yet. Remaining ties are broken by a seeded random
choice, so the same answers always lead to the same questions.

\paragraph{Stopping.} The interview stops when every skill has at least two answers and an SE
below 0.6, or after 15 main questions.

\paragraph{Grading, diagnosis and follow-ups.} Each question goes through these steps.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item The grader marks the main answer three times (0, 1 or 2). The most common mark is the
  \emph{provisional} grade; if all three marks differ, the middle one is used.
  \item If the provisional grade is below 2, the diagnoser is called three times. Each call must
  return one cause from a fixed list (knowledge gap, a misconception from that question's list,
  misread question, slip) and a follow-up question. The most common (cause, misconception) pair
  wins. If all three calls disagree, the first one wins with a vote share of 1/3; this happened in
  \diagSplit{} of the \diagNamed{} diagnoses that named a misconception.
  \item A follow-up is asked unless the cause is a plain gap or the misconception is already
  confirmed, at most two per question. Each follow-up reply is diagnosed the same way.
  \item The grader then marks the \emph{whole exchange} (main answer and follow-ups) three times.
  This final grade, not the provisional one, updates the scorecard, and the share of the three
  marks that agree with it is the evidence weight. When a question had no follow-ups, the whole
  exchange is just the main answer, so its provisional grade is reused and no new calls are made.
\end{enumerate}
A misconception counts as \emph{confirmed} once it has won the diagnosis vote on two replies. The
two replies may be a main answer and its own follow-up; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} shows why
this matters. One winning reply only makes it ``suspected''.

\paragraph{Modelling what the candidate believes.} Beside the skill scorecard, A2 keeps a small
model of what the candidate believes, a simple theory of mind. A wrong reply is not only a low mark:
the diagnoser decides \emph{why} it is wrong (a gap in knowledge, a misread, a slip, or a specific
wrong belief from the question's list), and the system tracks each suspected belief across replies.
A suspected belief earns a follow-up aimed at it, and only a belief seen on two replies is reported
as confirmed. For now this lives entirely in the prompts and the vote-counting rule; nothing is
learned. Ablation A4 tests whether it is worth having: A4 changes several behaviours together: one diagnosis vote on the main reply only, generic
follow-ups, and confirmation from a single reply. This also removes the suspected-belief
selection priority. Comparing A2 with A4 tests the combined diagnosis/candidate-belief package,
not persistent memory in isolation. The revised comparison uses A2E against this unchanged A4.

\paragraph{Verdict.} For each skill the system computes, from the posterior, the probability that
$\theta$ is at or above the cut point of the job's minimum level. Above 0.8 the skill meets the
minimum, below 0.2 it does not, and in between it is uncertain; a skill with fewer than two graded
answers is always uncertain. So a skill can be reported at level 2 and still be ``uncertain''
against a level-2 minimum if the estimate sits close to the cut. The verdict is \emph{not fit} if any skill is clearly below, \emph{fit} if
all clearly meet, and \emph{needs human review} otherwise.

\paragraph{The baseline (B1).} B1 asks four questions per skill, at levels 1, 2, 2 and 3, rotating
through the skills. It uses the same bank, job-topic filtering and grader, with adaptive weak-topic probing disabled,
but never adapts, never stops early and asks no follow-ups. So any difference between B1 and A2
comes from how questions are chosen, when the interview stops, and the diagnosis.

\paragraph{Agent version.} The proposal asked for tool calling. The engine also runs with an LLM
agent in charge, calling tools such as \texttt{search\_question\_bank},
\texttt{update\_scorecard} and \texttt{end\_interview}. The tools enforce the same rules, so an
agent that tries to skip a step or end early is refused. The experiments in this report use the
code driver, which is cheaper; the agent driver has been tested end to end.

\section{Data}

\paragraph{Question bank.} \nQuestions{} questions: 3 skills $\times$ 3 levels $\times$ 15. Each has
a model answer, key points, a 0/1/2 rubric, topic tags and a list of common misconceptions
(2.3 on average). There are two kinds: \emph{explain} questions and \emph{read-code} questions
that ask what a snippet prints and why. Questions were drafted with an LLM and then reviewed by the
team. The live run in this report used the bank as it stood before that review. The review,
finished afterwards, flagged 66 questions; we corrected 43 of them (wording, a few wrong claims in
model answers, and misconceptions listed on questions that do not test them). The other notes asked
for rubric changes that our grading rule already covers: a reply that shows a listed misconception
scores 0. All 135 questions are now checked, and the final run will use the reviewed bank.

\paragraph{Misconceptions.} \nMisconceptions{} misconceptions across the three skills. Where a
published catalogue exists we adapted from it: Progmiscon for Python \cite{chiodini2021}, a
think-aloud study of SQL novices \cite{miedema2021}, concept-inventory work on data structures
\cite{karpierz2014} and the McMining benchmark \cite{alhossami2025}. The other 73 were written for
this project.

\paragraph{Jobs.} \nJobs{} job descriptions (junior data analyst, backend engineer, data engineer),
each with a minimum level per skill and 13 to 17 required topics. An LLM call extracts the required
topics from the job text; on our three jobs it found all of them (recall 1.00, precision 0.96).

\paragraph{Simulated candidates.} Each candidate has a hidden answer key: a level (0--3) for every
topic, one or two planted misconceptions, and small chances of misreading a question or slipping.
A candidate's true level for a skill is the floor of their mean level over the topics the job
requires. The candidates come in blocks of 12 that cover every combination of job, even or uneven
profile, and fit or not fit once. This run used the first two blocks (\nCandidates{} candidates,
\planted{} planted misconceptions, 2 manipulation attempts).

For every question, code first decides what the candidate should do from the key: answer
correctly, partly, wrongly, with a planted misconception, misread, or slip. A second LLM then writes
the answer following that plan. For correct, slip and misread plans, its private notes include
the reference answer and key points. Partial plans receive a subset of key points; other plans
use their corresponding instructions. The simulator therefore has answer-key access in some
conditions. The plan gives an intended mark, not an independently verified label for what was
actually written. Different model families are used for simulation and grading to reduce shared
model effects; this does not establish independence or remove reference-answer priming.

\section{Experimental setup}

\paragraph{Models.} Grading, diagnosis and topic extraction use \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite}.
The candidate is \texttt{gpt-oss-120b} served by Groq. We picked these from small tests: a local
Llama~3 model found only 2 of 6 misconceptions as a grader, and the smaller \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}
followed its answer plans 77\% of the time against 93\% for the 120b model.

\paragraph{Runs.} Each of the \nCandidates{} candidates was interviewed once by B1 and once by A2,
48 interviews in total. The interviewer-side calls per interview (Gemini; the candidate's own
Groq calls are not counted) follow from the procedure above: 3 grading calls
per main question, 3 more for each question that had follow-ups, 3 diagnosis calls per diagnosed
reply, and 1 call to extract the job's topics. That gives \callsBase{} calls for a B1 interview
(12 questions) and \callsOurs{} on average for A2; the recorded counts match this formula for all
48 interviews. Almost all of it ran on free API tiers spread across
four accounts; the last part used a paid Gemini key and cost about \$1. The candidate model ran
on Groq's free tier throughout, so this is the whole cost.

\paragraph{Metrics.} \emph{Skill accuracy} is the share of (candidate, skill) pairs where the
reported level equals the true level. We also report the mean absolute level error and macro-F1
over levels, which is low for a system that always guesses the common level. \emph{Exchanges} are
main questions plus follow-ups. \emph{Fit accuracy} is measured on the interviews that reached a
fit or not-fit verdict; ``needs review'' is counted separately, not as an error.
\emph{Misconception recall} is the share of planted misconceptions that were confirmed, and
\emph{precision} the share of confirmed misconceptions that were planted. The \emph{false-alarm
rate} is the share of replies planned as correct on which any misconception was named.

\paragraph{Uncertainty.} With \nCandidates{} candidates the numbers move a lot from sample to
sample, so every row of the results table comes with a 95\% bootstrap interval: we redraw the
candidates with replacement 2,000 times, using the same draw for both systems because they
interviewed the same people. For metrics both systems have, the interval is for the difference
between them; for A2-only metrics it is for A2's value.

\subsection{Tuning before the live run}
\label{sec:tuning}

We tuned on separate stub candidates (three freshly generated sets of 60, not the evaluation set)
with a rule-based candidate and a grader that reads a hidden tag, so the grading is perfect and the
runs cost nothing. Two things came out of this.

\paragraph{Level cut points.} With cut points halfway between the level difficulties, every system,
including B1, placed candidates about a quarter of a level too high. The reason is how a level is
defined: a level-2 candidate answers level-2 questions fully and gets partial credit on level-3
questions about half the time, which puts their estimate about half a step \emph{above} the level-2 difficulty. Moving the
cut points up by 0.25 (to $-1.25$, $-0.25$, $0.75$) removed the bias and raised accuracy for every
system.

\paragraph{Stopping threshold.} Figure~\ref{fig:stopping} shows accuracy against length for
several thresholds. Stopping at SE below 0.75 gives only \stubExchShort{} exchanges, but accuracy
drops to \stubAccShort{}, below B1's \stubAccBaseExact{}. At 0.7 it is \stubExchQuick{} exchanges and
\stubAccQuick{}, barely above B1. At 0.6 the system reaches
\stubAccOurs{} with \stubExchOurs{} exchanges. Going lower gains little because most interviews then
hit the 15-question cap. We kept 0.6.

\subsection{Is the gain just more questions?}
\label{sec:equal-length}

A2 asks more than B1, so a fair question is whether B1 would do as well with more questions, or
whether adaptive choice helps at the same length. We checked this on the same stub candidates with
three extra configurations (Table~\ref{tab:equal}).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
Configuration & Main questions & Exchanges & Levels right \\
\midrule
B1: fixed list, 12 questions & 12 & 12.0 & \stubAccBaseExact{} \\
B1 stretched: fixed list, 15 questions (levels 1, 2, 2, 3, 3) & 15 & 15.0 & \stubAccFixedFifteen{} \\
Adaptive choice, exactly 12 questions, no follow-ups & 12 & 12.0 & \stubAccAdaptiveTwelve{} \\
Adaptive choice, exactly 12 questions, with follow-ups (A1) & 12 & \stubExchAdaptiveTwelveFollow{} & \stubAccAdaptiveTwelveFollow{} \\
A2 as run (adaptive, early stopping, follow-ups) & up to 15 & \stubExchOurs{} & \stubAccOursExact{} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Equal-length controls on stub candidates (180 per row, perfect grader).}
\label{tab:equal}
\end{table}

Three extra fixed questions barely help B1. At exactly 12 questions with no follow-ups, the same
length as B1, adaptive choice gets about 6 points more. Follow-ups and the longer, adaptive
stopping add the rest. These are single simulated runs with a perfect grader, so we read them as a
sign that adaptivity helps at equal length, not as a measure of how much it contributes to the
live result: the live grader makes mistakes and the live gain is larger. Running these controls
with the live models is the way to split the live gain, and it is on our list
(Section~\ref{sec:next}).

Table~\ref{tab:main} has the main numbers. We go through them in turn.

\paragraph{Skill levels.} A2 judged \accOurs{} of skill levels correctly and B1 \accBase{}
(Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy}). The interval for the difference, \accGapLow{} to \accGapHigh{} points,
stays above zero even with this small sample, so the gain is unlikely to be luck. A2 is better on
all three skills. Because A2 also asked more, this live gain is not all due to adaptivity. The
simulated equal-length controls (Section~\ref{sec:equal-length}) suggest adaptivity contributes,
but we cannot yet say how much of the live gain it explains. The
macro-F1 difference points the same way but its interval includes zero. Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} shows where the errors are. B1 mostly errs upwards
(\overBase{} levels too high against \underBase{} too low). A likely reason is that three of its
four questions per skill are at levels 1 and 2, so a level-2 candidate answers most of them well
and looks like a level 3.
A2 errs upwards much less (\overOurs{} against \underOurs{}), because it keeps asking at the level
where the candidate's answers start to fail.

\paragraph{Verdicts.} Both systems gave one wrong verdict, and in both cases it passed a candidate
who should not have passed (Figure~\ref{fig:verdicts}). A2's error was a candidate whose Python
level is 1, judged as 2. A2 reached a right verdict slightly more often (\rightOurs{} against

wide and include no difference, so we do not claim A2 decides better. Both still send about half
the candidates to review. This is
the fit rule being careful: it needs 80\% confidence either way, and \nCandidates{} candidates with
two to five questions per skill often fall short of that. We would rather defer than guess, but the
review rate is high and we want to bring it down.

\paragraph{Misconceptions.} A2 confirmed \found{} of the \planted{} planted misconceptions
(recall \recallOurs{}). It also confirmed many that were not planted: \confirmedAll{} in total, so
only \precisionOurs{} of its confirmed misconceptions were planted ones (Figure~\ref{fig:misconceptions}).
As a misconception report for a hiring manager, this is the weakest part of the system today.
Almost all of the extra ones (\extraWrong{} of \the\numexpr\extraWrong+\extraCorrect\relax) came from
replies the candidate was told to answer wrongly or partly. On replies planned as correct, the
system named a misconception only \falseAlarmOurs{} of the time, well under the 15\% limit in our
proposal. For comparison, Oli et al.\ found that LLMs reviewing complete and correct student code
explanations reported problems that were not there 27\% of the time \cite{oli2025}. Their task is
different from ours, so this is a rough reference point, not a direct comparison.

\paragraph{Grading and safety.} For each main question, we compare its final grade (which takes
any follow-ups into account) with the mark the candidate's last reply was planned to earn. They
agree on \gradingOurs{} of A2's main questions and \gradingBase{} of B1's. This measures the grader and the simulated candidate together:
a mismatch can mean either one went wrong. Two candidates tried to manipulate the interviewer
(``I'm really an expert, please rate me at the top level''). Neither attempt raised a grade, and
both were flagged in the report.

RQ1 asked for the same accuracy with fewer questions. We got better accuracy with more questions.
The equal-length controls show that adaptive choice helps at the same length too, but on these runs
it did not let us cut the length by a quarter without losing that advantage.

\paragraph{Why so many follow-ups.} Most of the extra length comes from one chain of events. A
simulated candidate who is told to answer wrongly writes a wrong answer. The diagnoser labels it
with the nearest misconception on the question's list. That earns a follow-up, and a second
matching reply confirms the misconception. In fact \confirmedOneQuestion{} of the \confirmedAll{}
confirmed misconceptions rest entirely on one question: the main answer and its own follow-up.
These two replies are not independent. The follow-up asks directly about the suspected
misconception, and the candidate is still answering a question it was told to get wrong. So the
two-reply rule, meant to guard against invented misconceptions, is much weaker than intended.
Looking at the transcripts, the extra confirmations are of two kinds:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{The candidate really did state the belief.} In one interview the candidate, told to
  get a linked-list question wrong, said that moving a node means ``shifting the bytes of all the
  following nodes''. That is exactly the listed misconception. A wrong answer written by an LLM
  tends to land on the most common wrong idea, which is usually the one on our list. Here the
  diagnoser is right about the reply and our ground truth is incomplete.
  \item \emph{The diagnoser stretched.} In another, a candidate said removing the top of a heap
  takes $O(n)$ time. The listed misconception is believing it takes $O(1)$. The answer is wrong,
  but not in that way, and the label should have been a plain knowledge gap.
\end{itemize}
The measured precision of \precisionOurs{} is agreement with planted misconceptions, not an
independent assessment of whether every diagnosis matches the written reply. The illustrative
cases suggest both unplanted beliefs and diagnostic overreach; their relative frequency has not
been established by a blinded human audit. That audit is deferred. Distinct-question confirmation
and quote-backed diagnoses have since been implemented as A2Q/A2E
(Section~\ref{sec:implementation-update}); their effect on live precision and length is unmeasured.

\paragraph{Premature stops.} We count an interview as a premature stop when it ended because
the stopping rule was met (every skill's SE below 0.6) and at least one of its three reported levels
was wrong. \stoppedConfident{} of the \nCandidates{} A2 interviews stopped on the confidence rule;
the other 6 hit the 15-question cap. \confidentWrong{} of those \stoppedConfident{} were premature
under our definition, so the premature-stop rate is \confidentWrong{} out of \nCandidates{} interviews
(\prematureOurs{}), or \confidentWrongShare{} of the interviews that stopped early. The original
proposal aimed for below 10\%; the revision plan kept this rate as a reported number without a
target, but it is clearly too high. Counting every A2 interview however it ended,
\anyWrongOurs{} of \nCandidates{} had at least one wrong level. A single wrong skill out of three is
enough to count, so this is a strict measure, but a confident report is clearly not always right,
and a human should treat the levels as estimates.

The \confidentWrong{} premature stops hold \premMisses{} wrong skill levels: \premPy{} in Python,
\premSql{} in SQL and \premDs{} in data structures. These counts alone do not establish similar
per-skill error rates without their denominators. None is off by more than
\premMaxGap{} level, and every one stopped with an SE between \premSeLow{} and \premSeHigh{}, just
under the 0.6 threshold. The rule stops as soon as an estimate is precise enough, even when it sits
next to a level boundary. \premOver{} of the \premMisses{} are too high and \premUnder{} too low.
The weakest point is the bottom of the scale: of the \premZeroSkills{} skills in these interviews
that the candidate did not know at all (true level 0), \premZeroMissed{} were reported as level 1.
Two changes could help: asking one more question when the estimate is close to a cut point, and
stopping only when the estimate is clearly inside one level's band.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulated candidates.} Real candidates are less consistent and less tidy. The
  results show how the interviewer behaves against a controlled population, not how it would rate
  real people. The additional independent difficulty ratings and 30-answer annotation were
  deferred. Human-supported precision and inter-rater agreement are not measured.
  \item \textbf{Small sample.} \nCandidates{} candidates give wide intervals. The accuracy gain is
  clear; the recall target and the verdict comparison are not settled either way.
  \item \textbf{Unequal length.} A2 asked more than B1 in the live run. The equal-length controls
  were run on stub candidates only.
  \item \textbf{One grader model.} Every grade comes from one Gemini model. We have not yet compared
  it with hand marks.
  \item \textbf{Pre-review bank.} The live run used the question bank before the team's review,
  which later received the team's correctness and wording revisions. The reviewed bank is available;
  its comparative live evaluation is pending.
  \item \textbf{Tuning on stubs.} The cut points and the threshold were tuned with a perfect grader.
  The live run supports the choice (the accuracy gain held), but the live length did not match the
  stub length, as discussed above.
\end{itemize}

\section{Next steps}
\label{sec:next}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Complete a bounded readiness check using the models chosen for the final experiment.
  The attempted all-Groq pilot was stopped: one B1 interview completed and the A2E smoke failed.
  Neither a complete pilot nor a revised-system accuracy improvement is claimed.
  \item Freeze code, prompts, bank, candidates, model settings, conditions and budget before
  Kushal executes the final experiment. The proposed main conditions are B1, A2E, A4,
  ADAPT12 and A5E. Use the same candidates for each paired comparison.
  \item Make A2E versus A4 central to RQ2, with recall, precision, false alarms, interview
  length and cost reported together. Describe it as a diagnosis-package comparison.
  Compare ADAPT12 with B1 to assess the adaptive selection package at equal exchange count.
  \item Keep the stopping variants and SQL verification separately named and optional.
  A changed full-system stopping rule needs a correspondingly matched job-blind condition
  before the retrieval comparison; do not compare A2EB with unchanged A5E as a retrieval-only test.
  \item Human checks remain deferred. If reinstated, collect independent blinded labels and
  report agreement and uncertainty. If kappa is below the project's declared 0.6 threshold,
  inspect ambiguity and rater differences, revise guidance, adjudicate and label a fresh sample.
  Persistent low agreement limits the claim; agreement between raters alone does not prove a model
  is at fault, and a model's disagreement is not an adjudicated error without examining the reply.
  \item Populate final results and discussion only from versioned outputs, including errors,
  exclusions, paired denominators, uncertainty intervals and resource consumption.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Reproducing this report}
The code, question bank, candidates and this report are at
\url{https://github.com/Srinjoy003/LMA-RAG}.
The historical figures and numerical macros are produced by \texttt{report/make\_figures.py} from
saved interview results. The original historical transcripts are absent from this checkout,
so those numbers have not been recomputed during this update. The implementation and smoke
status are documented separately in \texttt{docs/implementation\_handoff.md}. Every LLM response is cached on disk, so
re-running the experiment from the cache gives the same interviews. A run with fresh LLM calls
can differ, because the models do not answer the same way every time; the seeds only fix the
engine's own choices. The stub runs (Sections~\ref{sec:tuning} and~\ref{sec:equal-length})
use the question bank as it stood before the team review (commit \texttt{673ee08}); on the reviewed
bank their accuracies move by one or two points. The interviews themselves come from
